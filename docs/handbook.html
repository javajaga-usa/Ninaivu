PART ONE

The household

Household

You do not need to know anything about servers to use Ninaivu. This part is everything a family member or guest needs, and nothing else.

What Ninaivu is

Ninaivu is your family's photographs, videos and recordings, kept on one machine in your home and shown to everyone in the house through a web browser. There is no account to make, nothing is uploaded to a company, and no analytics leave the building. If the internet goes down, the library still works.

You open it like a website. On a phone or tablet, add it to your home screen once and it behaves like an app.

Two things are worth knowing up front, because they explain most of how it behaves:

  • Everything has a visibility. Every photograph is public, family, or hidden. What you see depends on which profile you came in as — see Visibility.
  • The management screens live somewhere else. There is a separate admin console on a different address. Nothing you can reach in the gallery can change the library.

Getting in

Opening Ninaivu lands you on a profile picker — Who's watching? Tap your face and you're in. What that costs you depends on how the admin set your profile up:

Tap to enterNothing at all. Right for a shared television or the family tablet.
PINFour to eight digits, shown as a small lock on your tile. Good for a teenager's profile on a shared screen.
Username + passwordThe full form, for anyone who wants it.
Just lookingA tile that needs no profile at all and shows only public media. The admin can switch it off.

Administrator profiles deliberately do not appear on this picker, and the picker will refuse to let anyone in as an admin even if asked directly. Admins sign in on the console instead.

You can always change your own display name, colour and profile picture, whatever your role. Pictures you upload are re-encoded to a 256 px square WebP, which strips any metadata and guarantees that what gets served back is actually an image.

Browsing the library

The gallery opens on everything you are allowed to see, newest first. Four layouts are available and they are a genuine preference, not a gimmick — justified suits mixed orientations, masonry suits tall photographs, grid suits scanning, filmstrip suits a single long roll.

Search

Type anything into the search box. What happens depends on which AI tier the admin turned on:

  • With CLIP running, an AI badge appears and results are ranked by how well the picture matches your words. “Red car at night” and “sunset over water” work as written — nobody had to tag anything.
  • With the light tier, search covers filenames, folders and simple composition tags such as wide, dark, monochrome.

The sidebar also carries whatever the library actually contains: discovered tags, years, folders, and — if faces are switched on — a row of people. Clicking any of them adds a removable filter chip. They stack, so “beach” + 2019 + a person is one click each.

Selecting several at once

Click the circle on a tile to select it, Shift-click for a range, or use Select all on any day heading. With a selection you can favourite or download in bulk; an admin also gets bulk visibility changes.

The month rail

Drag the strip down the right-hand edge to jump anywhere in the library by month. It works on a hundred thousand items as smoothly as on a hundred, because the whole layout is computed from one small payload rather than by loading pages as you scroll.

The viewer

Opening an item shows the whole photograph fitted to your screen, whatever shape your screen is. From there:

  • Zoom with the scroll wheel or a pinch; drag to pan; double-click to zoom straight to a point. 0 puts it back.
  • Page with the arrow keys or by swiping.
  • Find similar with S — visually similar shots, which is how you find the other eleven attempts at the same photograph.
  • Details with I — camera, lens, ISO, aperture, focal length, where the date came from, and location if the file carried one.
  • Rotate with R (Shift+R the other way). A rotation saved by a family member is saved for the whole household — the file on disk is never modified.

Live photographs and Google motion photos play their short video automatically; Ninaivu pairs the still and the clip during indexing and also detects motion photos embedded inside a single JPEG or HEIC.

It offers U for upload and M for a map. Neither key is bound — M selects the masonry layout, and the map opens from its button in the toolbar. Uploading is a button too.

People

If the admin has turned face grouping on, a People row appears in the sidebar showing a face and a name. Click one to filter the whole gallery to that person; it becomes a removable chip like any other filter.

Two things about it are worth understanding, because they are deliberate:

  • The counts are yours. A person's photograph count is how many photographs you are allowed to open. Two people in the same house can see different numbers for the same person, and neither can tell anything from the difference.
  • You cannot name anyone. Naming people, confirming matches and running detection are admin jobs on the console. In the gallery, People is read-only.

Faces smaller than 50 pixels are skipped on purpose, so a distant face in a crowd shot will not be picked up. That is the trade that keeps the ones it does show reliable.

Albums and favourites

Favourites are private to you. Starring a photograph puts it in your favourites and nobody else's — this holds even when several people share the same library folder. Guests cannot favourite; family members and admins can.

Albums are shared. Any family member can make one and add items to it. An album made by somebody else can still be edited by any family member if it predates Ninaivu recording owners; newer ones belong to whoever made them, and an admin can always edit.

Albums hold references, not copies. Deleting an album never touches a photograph.

Ambient Frame

Press K in the viewer and Ninaivu becomes a photo frame: full screen, chrome faded out, a slow Ken Burns drift across each picture, a clock in the corner, and a new photograph every eight seconds. Click anywhere or press Esc to come back.

It is the reason to keep an old tablet on the kitchen wall. Space gives you a plainer slideshow without the full-screen treatment.

Adding photographs

Family members and admins can upload straight from the gallery. Files land in a folder called Uploads inside the library — or, if you have been assigned a particular folder, inside that one. A name that already exists gets a timestamp added rather than overwriting anything, and the new file is indexed immediately rather than waiting for the next scan.

The default size limit is 512 MB per request. Guests cannot upload.

PART TWO

Running it

Admin

Everything on the admin console: getting a library indexed, deciding who sees what, and the three big tools — Faces, Archive and Cloud.

Install and first run

The launcher does the whole setup: it creates a virtual environment, installs what is missing, finds free ports and opens a browser.

# macOS / Linux python start.py ~/Pictures # Windows start.bat C:\Photos

Python 3.9 or later is the only prerequisite. On a Mac there is also a double-clickable Ninaivu.command; on Windows, start.bat.

Docker

# point MEDIA_DIR at your photographs first cp .env.example .env docker compose up -d

The first thing you'll be asked

On first launch there is no administrator, so the console asks you to create one. That account is refused once one exists — the setup route closes permanently. You can also create it without a browser:

python start.py ~/Pictures --admin dad:correcthorsebattery

What happens next

Ninaivu walks the folder, and the gallery becomes usable within seconds — long before the walk finishes. The passes run in this order, and each one is skippable and resumable:

  1. Index. One directory walk, then a thread pool for the expensive part (decode, thumbnail, hash). Files whose size and modification time are unchanged are skipped entirely, which is what makes a rescan fast.
  2. Duplicates. Perceptual hashes are compared and near-identical images grouped.
  3. AI tagging. Runs against thumbnails. Adds tags, captions and the embeddings that power natural-language search.
  4. Faces. Runs against originals, last, because it is the slowest and the least urgent.
Where the dates come from

EXIF DateTimeOriginal first, then container metadata, then a YYYY/MM/DD folder path, then a date in the filename, and only then the file's modification time. The viewer's details panel tells you which one was used — useful when a scanned print claims to be from 2019.

The two apps

Ninaivu runs as two applications in one process, on two ports. This is a security boundary, not a layout choice.

A session created on the family app cannot open the console even when it belongs to an administrator. Firewall port 3000 to your own machine and the console becomes unreachable from the network while the family app carries on serving the house.

Default binding

Ninaivu binds to 0.0.0.0 — every address the machine has — so the household can reach it with no flags. On a network you do not fully trust, add --admin-host 127.0.0.1 to keep the console local while the family app still serves the house. Ninaivu prints a warning at startup whenever the console is reachable beyond the machine.

Disabling versus deleting

Disable is reversible: the profile stays, keeps its favourites, and can be switched back on. Delete is final — the profile, its favourites, its picture and every open session go. Neither touches a single photograph; the library is shared, and what is being removed is only who may look at it.

You cannot delete the profile you are signed in with, and Ninaivu will not let you delete the last working administrator.

Sessions are revocable

Sessions are opaque server-side tokens. Disabling a profile, resetting its password or signing it out ends every session it has, on every device, immediately. Passwords and PINs use scrypt with per-value salts; sign-in is rate limited per address and per account, which matters most for four-digit PINs.

Library folders

Ninaivu can index several library folders — add as many as you like on the console's Library tab. Each person is then assigned one of them, or any folder inside one.

AssignmentWhat they see
All library foldersEverything indexed — the default
D:\PhotosThat whole library folder
D:\Photos\2024\SummerJust that subtree

One folder can be shared by any number of people — the family holiday album is the normal case. Sharing a folder shares nothing personal: favourites stay private to each person.

Assignment and visibility stack, and the narrower always wins. A guest given D:\Photos still sees only the public items in it. Admins are never confined. A profile pointed at a folder you later remove sees nothing, rather than silently falling back to the whole library.

The console's “What each role sees” panel resolves an assignment exactly as a real request does, so you can check a person before handing out a login.

Visibility

Every item sits at one of three levels.

Set it per item in the viewer, on a selection in the gallery, or on a whole folder from the console's Visibility tab.

Folder rules stick

A folder's setting is remembered as a rule, so files scanned into it later inherit the decision instead of quietly reappearing at the default level. Rescans never overwrite it: the database upsert deliberately does not touch the visibility columns.

Undo

Setting a folder rule rewrites the visibility of everything beneath it, and the per-item decisions it lands on would otherwise be gone. So the previous state is written down before it is replaced, and the Visibility tab offers an undo. This matters more than it sounds — choosing the wrong button on a whole library would otherwise make photographs an admin had deliberately hidden visible to the family, with nothing left that remembers they were hidden.

The audio rule

Sound recordings are administrator-only. Not by default — as a rule. It does not depend on an item's stored visibility, no folder rule lifts it, and there is no setting on the family app that turns it on.

The reasoning is about what actually ends up in a family's media folders. A library swept up from old drives and phone backups collects voice notes, voicemail, call recordings, dictation and meeting audio — things nobody chose to put in a gallery, and would never think to go and hide, because it did not occur to them they would be listed. A photograph is looked at on purpose. A sound file plays the moment somebody taps it, out loud, in a room with other people in it.

Photographs and video are untouched by this. An existing library is put right the first time this version opens it, so the rule applies to libraries indexed before it existed.

Faces

Ninaivu finds the faces in your photographs and groups them, so the gallery can be browsed by person. It runs entirely on your machine: no photograph, no face, and no measurement of a face is ever sent anywhere.

Turning it on

It is off by default. On Windows, start.bat now enables it for you; otherwise:

python start.py ~/Pictures --faces # or, equivalently export NINAIVU_FACES=1

Turning it on downloads nothing. Two ONNX models — a detector of about 232 KB and a recogniser of about 37 MB — are fetched only when you press the button in the console's Faces tab, and both are checked against a known SHA-256 before use. Until then the engine reports itself unavailable and the library behaves exactly as before. There is no new dependency: OpenCV was already required, and both models run through it.

The first run

  1. Open the console, go to Faces, press Download face models.
  2. Press Look for faces. It reads originals at roughly 25 photographs a second on a CPU. Progress is recorded per photograph, so stopping and restarting resumes.
  3. Groups appear under Groups waiting for a name. Type a name, press Enter.
  4. Use Review suggestions on a named person to answer the faces it was not sure about — one click each.
Why it reads originals

Thumbnails cap at 640 px, which puts a face in a group shot near 30 pixels — below the size where a measurement describes a person rather than a blur. That is why the face pass is separate from the AI tagging pass, which does use thumbnails.

How sure it has to be

Two faces are compared by the angle between their measurements. Measured against this model on real photographs:

ConditionSimilarityResult
Two photographs of the same person0.77Same
Same face, shrunk to 52 pixels0.87Same
Same face, greyscale0.88Same
Same face, JPEG quality 100.80Same
Same face, tilted 20°0.67Same
Two different people0.04–0.18Different

There is a wide empty band between those two groups, and every threshold sits inside it. Ninaivu is deliberately lopsided about which way it errs: putting a stranger into somebody's album is expensive because nobody goes looking for it, while not recognising somebody costs one click. So three things must all be true before a face is assigned without asking:

  1. it clears 0.50 against that person;
  2. it beats the next-closest person by 0.06 — the guard that keeps siblings, and parents and children, apart;
  3. it matches a face you actually confirmed for them, not merely their average.

Anything that fails one of those but still resembles somebody becomes a question in the review queue instead.

Only your answers teach it

A person is described by the faces you confirmed, never by the ones Ninaivu matched on its own. If automatic matches fed back in, one wrong guess would pull the description toward the wrong face and make the next wrong guess likelier — an album would slowly fill with a stranger while every individual step looked reasonable.

“No” is permanent. A rejection is remembered and never asked again, and a face you have rejected for anybody is never auto-assigned to anybody afterwards — having been ambiguous once is reason enough to keep asking.

The rule that makes grouping work

A face joins a group only if it resembles every corner of that group, not just its average. Without that, A resembles B and B resembles C, and a chain of individually reasonable links quietly walks one group across three different people. That single rule is the difference between face grouping that works and face grouping that has to be undone by hand.

Who can see it

A face is only ever reachable through its photograph, so every visibility rule and folder assignment that governs the photograph governs the face automatically. In particular: somebody who appears only in hidden photographs is absent from the People list entirely — not present with a zero beside their name, which would itself give away that they are in the library. A face crop from a photograph you may not open returns 404.

The Archive tab

Most households do not have a library yet. They have four external drives, two old phone backups and a box of SD cards, with the same photograph on three of them. The Archive tab turns that into something worth pointing the library at.

Give it source folders and a destination, and it copies every photograph, video and audio file into one archive filed by capture date:

MasterArchive/ 2016/02/03/IMG_0001.jpg 2019/10/10/IMG_0042.jpg undated/CLIP.DAT

What it promises

  • Nothing is re-encoded. Files are streamed byte for byte. A JPEG that goes in comes out with identical bytes — same pixels, same EXIF, same everything.
  • Nothing is deleted, ever. Sources are opened read-only. Duplicates are recorded and left exactly where they are.
  • Nothing is trusted unverified. Every file is SHA-256 hashed as it is read, then the archived copy is re-read from disk and hashed again. Only a match counts as verified.
  • Nothing is left half-written. Copies land on a temporary name and are renamed into place once complete. Kill the process mid-run and the archive holds whole files or nothing.
  • Drive disconnects recover safely. If a removable source or destination goes away mid-file, the partial copy is discarded and the run waits. Put the same volume back and the walk restarts automatically, stepping over every verified file; a different volume at the same mount point is refused.
  • Nothing is skipped in silence. A file with an unrecognised extension has its first bytes read and identified by content, so IMG_0042 with no extension and a JPEG renamed .txt are both archived.
  • Start is also Resume. Progress lives in SQLite.

The archive engine also runs a guardian that samples archived files, re-hashes them and compares against what was recorded — a real integrity check, and one that reports missing, changed and unreadable files separately. It also warns when the destination drops below 10% free space.

Cloud backup

The console's Cloud tab keeps a second copy of the library in one folder in a Google Drive account you choose. It is deliberately narrow.

  • One way. Files go out. Nothing is ever downloaded, changed or removed on this machine, and there is no code path that could do it.
  • Once. Every file is uploaded exactly once, recorded against the library folder and the path inside it — never a database id, because ids get recycled and a recycled id inheriting somebody else's “already sent” is how a file goes missing silently.
  • A deletion in Drive is not an instruction. Clear space in Drive and Ninaivu does not notice, does not re-upload, and does not treat the absence as a request to delete anything here.
  • One folder. Ninaivu finds or makes a single top-level folder and works inside it. It cannot create a folder or start an upload without a parent inside that folder.
  • Hidden items stay here. Hiding a photograph from your own household is not consent to put it on Google. There is a switch, and it is off.
  • Pausing is instant. The stop flag is checked between chunks, so pausing a 4 GB video takes effect in seconds and costs nothing.

Uploads run one file at a time on one background thread — not because parallel uploads would be hard, but because the thing being shared is a domestic broadband connection. Four at once do not finish sooner; they just make the house unable to use the internet.

Speed limit and hours

Two controls, both read while an upload is running so a change applies to the transfer in front of you. A speed limit in KB/s (0 means none), where the chunk size comes down with the cap so the ceiling is approached smoothly rather than by sending eight megabytes flat out and then sleeping. And the hours it may run, as two local times; 22:00–07:00 crosses midnight as you would expect.

An upload still going when the hours end stops where it is and carries on the next night. The resumable session is kept, and the file is recorded as still waiting rather than as a failure — counting an interrupted night as a failed attempt would turn a large video into a permanently failed file after five uneventful nights, with no cause anybody could find.

Setting it up

Ninaivu cannot ship a Google client secret — a secret inside an application anybody can unzip is not a secret, and a shared one would put every household behind one quota and one revocation. So you make your own, once:

  1. Open console.cloud.google.com and make a project.
  2. Enable the Google Drive API.
  3. Under Credentials, create an OAuth client of type Web application — not Desktop app, which only permits localhost redirects.
  4. Add the authorised redirect URI the Cloud tab shows you (there is a Copy button).
  5. Paste the client ID and secret into the Cloud tab, then Connect.

The scope requested is drive.file: permission to see and manage only the files this application creates. A Ninaivu token cannot read a single thing in your Drive that Ninaivu did not put there. The refresh token is kept in one owner-only file, never sent to the browser, and never appears in a log or an API response.

Not yet run against Google itself

The client is exercised against a protocol-faithful stand-in — resumable sessions, 308 with a short Range, expired sessions, 429/5xx backoff, revoked permissions. That proves the client implements the protocol it was written against. It does not prove a real upload works, and the distinction is worth keeping in mind for the first run.

Deleting and the recycle bin

Deleting from Ninaivu does not delete anything. Files are moved into a folder called _deleted inside their own library folder, and the index row is removed only once the file has actually moved. Two deletions of the same filename both survive — the second gets a numbered suffix.

The bin is a plain folder. You can open it in Explorer or Finder, and you can restore from the console's Folders tab. Nothing expires on a timer; emptying it is your decision.

Share links

Family members and admins can create a link to a single item or a whole album. Each link carries:

tokenA 16-byte URL-safe random token — the link itself.
expires_in_daysOptional. Omit for a link that does not expire.
passwordOptional, hashed with scrypt like any other password.
view_countRecorded, so you can see whether a link has been opened.

Links are listed and revocable from the console. Revoking one is immediate.

Duplicates and integrity

Duplicates

Ninaivu computes a perceptual hash for every image and groups those within a Hamming distance of 6 — close enough to catch the same photograph saved twice at different qualities or sizes, far enough not to group two similar sunsets. The gallery has a Duplicates view; grouping is reporting only, and nothing is deleted for you.

The library scrubber

The console offers a bitrot scrubber that walks the library and hashes every file.

This does not currently detect bit rot

Verified in the source: the scrubber computes each file's hash and stores it as both the expected and the actual value, then always records the status verified. Because it never compares against a previously recorded hash, a file that silently changed between two runs is reported as verified both times. The only thing it flags is a file it cannot read at all, or one that has gone missing.

The Archive tab's guardian is different, and does compare properly. If you want real integrity checking today, that is the one to rely on. This is listed with the other findings in Accuracy notes.

PART THREE

Operations

Operator

Getting it onto the network safely, keeping it running, and what to do when it misbehaves.

The home network

By default Ninaivu listens on every address the machine has. To confirm, and to see what to type on other devices, read the startup banner:

on your network: family → http://192.168.1.24:5000 ← phones and tablets admin → http://192.168.1.24:3000

Give the machine a static or reserved IP so that address does not change, and open the port in the OS firewall the first time — Windows asks; on Linux sudo ufw allow 5000/tcp.

A name instead of an address

Ninaivu announces itself on the network, so devices can use http://ninaivu.local:5000. Change it with --name family-photos, or switch it off with --no-mdns. It needs the optional zeroconf package; without it Ninaivu says so once and carries on serving by address.

The name is answered by the devices themselves, not by your router, so a device has to speak mDNS. Apple devices always do, Windows 10 and later do, most Linux desktops do via Avahi, and Android is patchy below Android 12. Anything left out can still use the IP address.

The alternative that always works is a static DNS entry on the router — look for DHCP reservation, Static lease, Local DNS or Host names. If you run Pi-hole or AdGuard Home, add it under Local DNS records.

When other devices see nothing

python tools/netcheck.py

Run it on the machine serving the library. It tells you whether Ninaivu is listening beyond this machine, which of its addresses to type (a machine with a VPN or a virtual-machine adapter has several, and only one is on the family's Wi-Fi), and the firewall command for your platform. In order of likelihood the cause is: the host flag is wrong, the firewall is blocking the port, the phone is on a Guest network, or the router has client isolation switched on.

Reaching it from outside the house

Use a VPN. Never a forwarded port.

HTTPS

python start.py ~/Pictures --host 0.0.0.0 --https

No certificate authority will vouch for 192.168.1.24, and a bare self-signed certificate greets the household with a full-page security warning — which teaches everyone to click through security warnings. So Ninaivu issues its own small CA, keeps the key on the machine, and signs a certificate with it. Install the CA once per device and every browser in the house gets an ordinary padlock.

DeviceHow
iPhone / iPadOpen https://<ip>:5000/ninaivu-ca.crt in Safari → Allow → Settings → Profile Downloaded → Install. Then Settings → General → About → Certificate Trust Settings and switch Ninaivu on.
AndroidOpen the same URL → Settings → Security → Encryption & credentials → Install a certificate → CA certificate.
WindowsDouble-click the file → Install Certificate → Local Machine → Trusted Root Certification Authorities.
macOSDouble-click → Keychain Access → System → set to Always Trust.
Linuxsudo cp ninaivu-ca.crt /usr/local/share/ca-certificates/ && sudo update-ca-certificates

The CA lasts ten years; the server certificate lasts 397 days — the longest Apple devices accept — and Ninaivu renews it automatically as it nears expiry. Because the CA is unchanged, nothing needs re-installing. Move the machine to a different subnet and the certificate is quietly re-issued for the new address.

Already have a real certificate? Use it, and none of the above runs:

python start.py ~/Pictures --cert fullchain.pem --key privkey.pem

The session cookie is marked Secure automatically whenever the connection is encrypted, whichever route you take.

Running it 24/7

The repository ships deployment files for the common cases: deploy/systemd/, deploy/caddy/, deploy/nginx/, deploy/windows/install-service.ps1, plus a Dockerfile and docker-compose.yml. docs/operations/production.md in the repository covers each in depth.

Two things matter more than which one you pick:

  • Keep the console off the network. Set --admin-host 127.0.0.1 and reach it over SSH or from the machine itself. Anyone who can reach the console sees its sign-in page.
  • Let the machine sleep, or don't. Ninaivu keeps the machine awake while it runs, so a scan or an upload is not cut off half way. --allow-sleep turns that off.

Performance

Measured in headless Chromium against a synthetic 100,000-item library:

Grid usable after load600 ms
Tiles in the DOM50 (of 100,000)
Full layout recompute45 ms
One virtualised render pass2.5 ms median, 3.8 ms p95
Continuous scrolling60 fps

Three design choices get it there. One compact request describes the whole library — /api/segments returns a small tuple per item, grouped by day, enough for a pixel-exact layout and a true scrollbar, gzipping to a few hundred KB at 100k items. Thumbnails are addressed by id, derivable from that payload, so drawing a screen needs no second round trip. And the DOM holds only what you can see — cells recycle through a pool, position with transform, index into 600 px buckets, and scroll work is coalesced into one requestAnimationFrame.

Tuning

--workers NScanner threads. Defaults to the CPU count, capped at 8.
--ai offSkip tagging entirely on a weak machine.
--no-watchStop watching the library for changes; scan on demand instead.
NINAIVU_MIN_MEDIA_BYTESIgnore files below a size. Defaults to 60 KB, which skips icons and sprites.

Backup and recovery

Your photographs are the thing that matters, and Ninaivu never writes to them — everything it generates lives in the state directory. That means two different backup jobs:

  • The media folders are yours to back up as you always would. The Cloud tab is one option; a second drive is another.
  • The state directory holds the index, thumbnails, avatars, settings and the TLS certificate. Losing it costs you a rescan and your profiles — not a photograph.
python tools/backup_restore.py --help python tools/db_maintenance.py --help python tools/verify_recovery_report.py

A hot backup of the index is safe: SQLite runs in WAL mode.

Faces tab says unavailableEither the models are not downloaded, or OpenCV is missing the face modules. The tab prints the exact reason.

A guest sees too muchUse the console's “What each role sees” panel — it resolves the assignment exactly as a real request does.
Audio will not playWorking as intended. Sound recordings are administrator-only and no setting lifts it.

The state directory

Everything Ninaivu generates lives in ~/.ninaivu (override with NINAIVU_STATE_DIR). Your media folders are never written to.

~/.ninaivu/ index.db # SQLite, WAL mode, FTS5 config.json # saved settings thumbs/ # 256px and 640px WebP derivatives faces/ # 112x112 face crops models/ # the two ONNX face models avatars/ # profile pictures ninaivu-ca.crt # the local certificate authority

The one exception is the recycle bin, which lives as _deleted inside each library folder — deliberately, so a deleted file stays on the same drive as the library it came from rather than being copied across volumes.

How it's built

ninaivu/ __init__.py # app factories: shared services, one face per port __main__.py # CLI; runs both servers, with port fallback config.py # layered config: defaults → config.json → env → flags auth.py # roles, scrypt passwords and PINs, sessions, scope db.py # SQLite index (WAL, FTS5), visibility, per-user state scanner.py # incremental parallel scan, folder rules, watcher media.py # EXIF, ffprobe, thumbnails, pHash, BlurHash ai.py # tiered engine, zero-shot vocabulary, semantic search faces.py # YuNet detection, SFace embeddings, model download facematch.py # the matching policy - pure vectors, no OpenCV faceindex.py # the passes: detect, regroup, confirm, suggest api.py # media API (both faces) accounts_api.py # sign-in, profiles, avatars, people admin_api.py # console-only: overview, folder tree, role preview archive_api.py # console-only: the Archive tab's endpoints archive/ # the consolidation engine, carried over whole cloud/ # Drive client, queue, rate and window limits static/js/ # layout · grid · viewer · accounts · app · admin · archive · faces

A note on the face modules being three files rather than one: faces.py knows about pixels, facematch.py knows about vectors, and db.py knows about rows. Keeping the matching policy free of any OpenCV import is what lets the rules that decide identity be tested exhaustively on synthetic vectors, with no model on disk — which is the only way to be confident about them.

Tests

pip install pytest && python -m pytest tests -q

1,030 tests. The ones that matter most are in test_roles.py, test_two_apps.py, test_library_folder.py, test_member_folders.py and test_delete_profile.py: every capability asserted from both directions — the role that should have it and the roles that should not — plus port isolation, PIN and picker behaviour, scope and visibility stacking, and the traversal, allowlist and header assertions.

test_archive_engine.py runs real consolidation jobs over real files and then looks at what is on disk. test_cloud_sync.py drives the Drive client through a protocol-faithful stand-in and spends most of its length on the promise whose failure would be unrecoverable. test_faces.py tests the matching rules on synthetic measurements at controlled distances, then the access rules, then the engine itself where the models are present.

Two environment-dependent tests

test_upright.py needs opencv-python-headless<5; OpenCV 5 removed the Haar cascade classifier the orientation detector is built on. test_netinfo.py needs a routing table it can parse, which containers often do not have.

Accuracy notes

This guide was written by reading the source rather than the existing documentation. Four places where the two disagree, recorded here rather than quietly papered over:

The library scrubberDoes not detect bit rot. It stores each file's freshly computed hash as both the expected and the actual value and always records verified, so it never compares against a previous run. Only unreadable or missing files are flagged. The Archive tab's guardian does compare correctly.
The in-app shortcut listLists U for upload and M for a map. U is not bound to anything, and M selects the masonry layout — the map opens from its toolbar button.
The README test countSaid 877; the suite collects 1,030. It was stale before the face work added 47 more. Now corrected in the README.
Line endings in the working tree139 files carry CRLF while the repository stores LF. This is local drift, not something a fresh clone sees, but it is what makes test_mac_launcher.py fail locally — a shell script with CRLF will not run correctly on macOS.

Everything else in this guide — the role matrix, the visibility rules, the flags, the environment variables, the endpoint lists, the archive and cloud guarantees, the face thresholds — was read from the implementation and matches it.
